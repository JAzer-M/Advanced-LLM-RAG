They determine it via two comparisons of training effort: layer depth and convergence speed.

They compare training effort from two perspectives — the number of layers and the convergence speed of the encoder and decoder [10]. For depth, they vary the number of encoder and decoder layers respectively, reasoning that deeper components yielding more gains indicate a task needing more representation capability and thus harder to learn; adding more encoder layers achieves larger improvements than adding more decoder layers [9][2]. For convergence, they fix the parameters of a well-trained decoder (encoder), update only the encoder (decoder), and find the decoder converges faster than the encoder [2][7]. From these two results — more gains from deeper encoder layers and the encoder needing more training time — they conclude the encoder handles a more difficult task than the decoder, i.e., the decoder's task is easier [8].
